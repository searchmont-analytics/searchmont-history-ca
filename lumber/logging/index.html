SEARCHMONT USED TO BE LUMBERJACKS'STOP-OVER Recalls Some of Oldtimers; 1941 More About Lumbarmen By S. B.DICKINSON Just finished reading Mollie Malone's column about the moose and the cherry tree. But that ain't the way Iheered it Mollie. Ialso knew the Stover spoken of when I was a kid of thirteen it was at this age I started to camp John R.(as Stover was known was then) superintendent for the E.F. Loud company who owned and operated a narrow gauge railway Cown as the Au Sable and Northwestern about sixty miles north of Bay City. Imet him again in the camps back of Blind River, his son, whom we all know as Stub was elected after the previous world war as M.P.P.from Blind River. The James Cosgrove mentioned was at Wild which I think is now Northland in the year of 1904 as foreman in the bush. Mike a brother of Jimwasone of our old fighting shanty men. Charlie Keenan who until his death managed the Royal Hotel at the corner of Gore and Queen was at this time proprietor of the Searchmont hotel. -一 This I think was the year after the Steel Plant strike and we all received our checks monthly from the Algoma Commercial company, at least I think this was the name they operated under. In order to get our checks cashed we must purchase something and what more could we purchase at an hotel than liquor.I was a cookee at this time and our cook was a real booze artist. When pay day arrived we gave the tote teamster our checks, that is the cook, myself and another cookee. Also a gallon container which was filled for $3.When that arrived it meant, we the cooke es would get breakfast and dinner the following day and as most of the gang sent checks out and also received liquid refreshments abig celebration was the result. As often happened a big fight was in progress, and possibly a portion of the crew wound up in Searchmont to stay until the month's wages were gone. IfI remember right the camps in this vicinity that winter numbered four under the foreman sip of Cameron Beck for whom Iworked, George Pyette, Charlie Gaudette and Tom Scott. The first two mentioned have since passed away. Billy Parks who died a year ago Sault Daily Star Feb 25 and who acted as foreman employed by the Lake Superior Paper Company and in later years by the Abitibi was at this time a teamster and top-notch hook man. - Searchmont though only bcasting one hotel was a stop over place for this Jack going in and coming out. Bright-eyed men, tall and straight slike the trees with which they labored coming in from the camps by the old tote road shouldered their way 1through the milling crowd up to the one bar the town boasted to geta glow on while waiting for the train twhich would carry them to the Soo. -O I don't think there was any vocation in life unless it be the builders of railroads that allows one to view more beautiful scenery than that of the camp life, yet it was all unobserved by them. It was their natural environment and Nature's canvas depicting beautiful sunrises or sunsets meant nothing to them. Yet they had little or no opportunity to view any of Nature's expositions as the rise and setting of the sun found them busy, heads bowed to their tasks. The beauty spots on the river were the most dangerous. We might take for instance the Serpent back of Blind River and pick out what was known as the Improvements. First there was the Chute from the upper dam that emptied its waters into the pond just above what was known as the Blind Dam, here there was an eddy on each side of the stream, the larger one on the right hand side facing up stream. The Chute of possibly a hundred feet or more at an incline of 35 or more degrees, shot logs down at express train speed and dove down endwise out of sight; and up again with such force they nearly left the water. From here they were carried to another chute a short distance below where they were again hurled through a churning cauldron toat last float into the quiet waters below. This place where waters leaped into the air for many feet and broke to fall back in a shimmering spray, rainbow hued in the rising sun which lanced through the pearly swirlsof mist resembling silken bridal robes on the spreading out of a vast opalescent curtain. Now the river that at one time overflowed its banks at flood time has deteriorated as have the pine tree forests. The hills are nude, their gaunt heads covered only by driet up moss as lifeless as a dead man's beard.
