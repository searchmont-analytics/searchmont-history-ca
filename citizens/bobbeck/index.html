Searchmont, Bob Beck's home for more than seven decades...settled in the broad valley of the Goulais River north of the Sault...its industrial focal point, the Weldwood Mill whose water-tower is dwarfed by the surrounding tree covered mountains...the slopes and chalets on its southern outskirts, well known to ski enthusiasts... On a sultry summer night, in the wilderness of the Algoma district, most of the resident activity is centred on the laundromat or the hotel. The main diversion for an out-of-towner would be to go down to the Algoma Central Railway tracks and listen to the rails as they groan and bang while cooling off from a day in the sun. Known as "the hermit", Bob Beck could be seen on such a night, alone with his thoughts, as he made his way through the twilight. He used to shuffle along the gravel shoulier of the Searchmont highway From the railroad track, he followed the curve in the road past the general store and off toward the river. His face, at least as much as was visible from under the battered old hat, was black with filth. His patched and re patched clothes had lost their original color under a uniform layer of gray dirt. He turned up the river road, bent slightly under the heavy sack strung over his shoulder.A dusty half-mile later, he arrived at a shack set back slightly in the woods. The hillsides were gold and green and silent with the evening, as Bob Beck opened the door and was swallowed up by the dark interior of his home. of his bequest, it would have been no surprise to the hermit's former neighHis shack, however, gave no hint of its owner's affluence. It was half hidden behind piles of wood and a huge heap of old tires. The shack was erected on the property of a school teacher who had left the area but continued to pay the taxes. Bob made most of the contents. The stove was a series of tin cans and drums he had assembled. All of his utensils were fashioned out of scraps of wood and metal..He could be seen, almost daily, visiting the local junk piles; he found uses for things anybody else would discard. Bob's personal appearance wasa product of his frugal lifestyle. The pile of tires outside his shack accounted for the colour of his skin--he burnt them in his stove. This kept him black especially in the winter when the temperatures would drop to 50-below zero, preventing proper yen til ation and rendering bathing impossible.

Bob Beck first came to the Searchmont area in 1906 when he secured a job as a surface worker for Superior Mines. The mines, now known as Copper Crest, are situated near Wabos, another Algoma village about l0 miles from Searchmont. At that time, there were 50 men employed at the site. The highest wages were 25 cents an hour. Bob, however, as a surface worker, would have received only $1.75fora10-hour day. During the First World War, Bob spent three years in the army. He joined the ll9th Battalion of the Sault Ste. Marie Regiment. Drafted into the 42nd Battalion in France, he served as a sniper as well as an operator of a Lewis gun. a lot of close shaves." His battle experience, however, left him without his sense of taste, the fact which explains his ability to consume the awful conglomerations which he used to cook on his home-made stove. In 1916, while at training camp in England, Mr. Beck placed first in a target-shooting match. Princess Louise, sister of King George V, presented him with a silver-tipped cane. Through the years this cane passed from hand to hand among various residents of the Searchmont area. When the war was over, Bob returned to Searchmont in order to maintain a postoffice address. (The Searchmont Post Office is the only one within 30 miles to the south and 100 miles in any other direction.) But, not all of his business could be conducted through the mails. For many years it was his custom to make a monthly trip into the Sault in order to do his banking and other business. Onefall day while he was waiting for the bus, he was clipped by a hit-and-run driver. . He maintained his silence and got onto the bus but, by the time he arrived in the Sault, he could hardly walk because of a hip injury. A policeman, taking him for a drunk, picked him up as he staggered along Queen Street. by the police. Even though he used to take off one layer of grime before visiting the Sault, he still looked like a tramp. The police, threatening him with vagrancy charges, thought that he was fooling when he demanded that they contact his banker. Finally they relented. The banker rushed over and secured the release of his friend and client. After ahasty explanation of the elderly man's financial status and physical condition, the threats were withdrawn and Bob was rushed off to hospital for treatment. Although no bones were broken, he was kept in hospital for two weeks and· spent the balance of the winter with friends in the Sault.A much cleaner hermit returned to his shack the following spring only to find that vandals had ransacked his belongings and his rifles had been stolen. Through all his years in Searchmont, Bob had various types of employment. He worked at bush camps and lumber mills besides doing a great deal of trapping and gardening. By the time he had left Searchmont to enter a retirement home in the Sault, he was over 80 years old and estimated that he had trapped scores of wolves..In fact, his pride and joy, a five-string home-made banjo, was faced with wolf skin. o he inherited his money from a sister, Mrs. Olive May Van Schoonhoer, who lived in Detroit up until her death in l965.Others maintain that his fortune was based on the sale of the original family homestead in Southern Ontario, augmented through the years by profits from real estate holdings in and around Sault Ste. Marie. Although secretive in his habits, Bob was moved by the suffering of other people. Up until the last few years of his life, Bob used to go to the Algoma Central Railway station with an old bear skin. Whenever a tourist-filled train would pull in, he would drape the skin over himself and parade around the platform to advertise the fact that this was the big bear district of Algoma. He e used to have shacks scattered throughout the surrounding mountains. He also planted a good number of apple trees off in the woods. Every fall he would carry the harvest out on his back. He would peddle some of the apples to his neighbors. About once a year, the old hermit would drop in on each of his friends to reminisce, play his banjo and sing a few songs. In return, his neighbors provided him with refreshment and second-hand newspapers to read to keep up with the times. On Sunday, July 25,1965, to celebrate his 80th birthday, Bob's close friends gave him a party at the home of Lee Hult in Searchmont. It was his first birthday party in 65 years. The Sault Star reported that Bob took the opportunity to play several selections on his banjo including The March of the 42nd Battalion, one of his owm compositions. Sir Adam Beck, the great uncle of the Searchmont hermit should be well known to Londoners. He was a mayor of London from 1902 to 1904and an MLA from 1905 until 1919.He also established the Hydro Electric Power Commission of Ontario of which he was the chairman until his death in 1925.The innate Beck spirit for pioneering ran strong whether in the relatively civilized surroundings of Southwestern Ontario or in the wilderness of the Algoma district of northern Ontario.

Eventually big boilers were moved into the mine and men were hired to cut cord wood to run the new steam-operated machines. Arailroad ands was also built intn the mine but the price of copper dropped so drastically that soon all operations were halted. Bob Beck spent three years in the Army during the First World War. He joined the 119th Battalion of the Sault Ste. Marie Regiment but was drafted to the 42nd in France. Some of his duty in France was as a sniper but mostly he was on Louis guns, a type of machine gun. He said "Iwasnever wounded but sure had lots of close shaves.' In 1916, while still in England at a training camp with his original battalion, he entered a target shooting competition and won the shoot off. He was presented with a silver-tipped cane by Princess Louise, a sister of King George V. The cane is still in a home at Searchmont. Quite a few years ago, Bob recalls that he gave the cane to Dave Jones who at that time lived at the Charlie Thorp home. Upon his death the cane passed to C. Wilson. When Mr. Wilson died a few years ago the cane again passed into new hands. In the years since the end of the First World War, Mr. Beck has maintained a home at Searchmont so as to have a Post Office address.
